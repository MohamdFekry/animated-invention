Lesson 2

Big buckets should speak louder than tiny buckets.

A 500-task hour tells us much more about the customer experience than a 5-task hour. Task weighting lets volume matter without pretending every task is independent.

Tiny example

5 tasks at 60 min vs 500 tasks at 20 min.

The unweighted average is 40 min. The task-weighted average is only 20.4 min because most tasks came from the busy, fast bucket.

Plain English

Weighting answers “what did most tasks experience?”

Unweighted averages answer “what did the average bucket look like?” That can be useful, but it can overreact to a tiny bucket with a strange value.

weighted average = sum(metric x tasks) / sum(tasks)

Bucket weighting playground

Two buckets, same metric, very different volume.

Small bucket
5 tasks, 60 min
Busy bucket
500 tasks, 20 min
Unweighted 40.0
Task-weighted 20.4
Difference 19.6
The small bucket has a loud metric but very little volume. Task weighting keeps it from dominating the business view.

Try this

Increase the small bucket to 250 tasks.

Once the small bucket is no longer small, the weighted average starts moving toward the unweighted average.

Careful

Weighting is not task-level inference.

We still respect switchback buckets. The weight only says how much business volume each bucket represents.

Product lesson

Show the weighted result by default.

It is usually closer to the question the business asks: what did customers and tasks actually experience?
