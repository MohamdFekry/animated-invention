Experiment analysis is mostly about asking fairer questions: are groups comparable, how noisy is the result, and when should we trust a small slice?

Course map

Six short lessons, each with its own playground.

The pages are ordered from most practical to most advanced. You can read them in order, or jump straight to the concept that feels fuzzy.

  1. 1 Fleet mix Why the global result can be smaller than every fleet result.
  2. 2 Task weighting Why busy buckets should count more than tiny buckets.
  3. 3 CUPED Why comparing to historical expectation reduces noise.
  4. 4 Confidence intervals Why a point estimate needs an uncertainty range.
  5. 5 Adjusted regression Why we compare treatment and control under similar conditions.
  6. 6 MLM Why small fleets should be pulled toward the overall pattern.

How to study

Use the sliders like questions.

Do not try to guess the answer first. Move one control, watch one number change, and ask: what did this control represent in the experiment?

What good looks like

A result should explain itself.

A strong E2 result should tell you the effect, the uncertainty, whether the groups were comparable, and where fleet-level differences matter.

Final rule

Global numbers are not enough.

The useful question is not just “did treatment win globally?” It is “where did it help, where did it hurt, and how sure are we?”
