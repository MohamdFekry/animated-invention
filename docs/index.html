How to use this

Each lab teaches one habit.

The goal is not to memorize formulas. The goal is to notice what can fool a raw experiment comparison, then choose the simplest adjustment that makes the conclusion easier to trust.

Lab 1: task mix

Why can every fleet improve, but the global result looks smaller?

Global CPO is a weighted average. If treatment gets more volume from a high-cost fleet, the global average is pulled upward even when treatment improves inside every fleet.

Try this: increase Marina's treatment share. The fleet-level treatment effects stay fixed, but the global effect gets less impressive.

Lab 2: weighted averages

A tiny bucket should not speak as loudly as a busy bucket.

E2 can still analyze fleet-hour buckets. Task weighting simply says a 500-task hour represents more customer experience than a 5-task hour.

Try this: raise the small bucket's task count. The weighted average will move toward the unweighted average.

Lab 3: CUPED

Compare against what each bucket was expected to do.

CUPED turns raw outcomes into residuals: actual minus historical baseline. That makes a dinner-peak treatment bucket comparable with a quiet-hour control bucket.

Try this: make the treatment baseline higher. Raw comparison still looks scary, but the CUPED residual can show treatment performing better than expected.

Lab 4: confidence intervals

A result is only useful when you can see its uncertainty.

The interval gets tighter when bucket count increases or noise falls. If the interval crosses zero, the data still allows a no-effect or opposite-effect story.

Try this: lower bucket count or increase noise. The point estimate stays the same, but the conclusion becomes less decisive.

Lab 5: adjusted regression

Raw averages can confuse treatment with peak-hour imbalance.

Adjusted regression compares treatment and control inside similar conditions. In this one-fleet example, the adjusted effect stays close to the within-hour differences.

Lab 6: MLM

Small fleets need humility, not overconfidence.

A multilevel model partially pools small-sample fleet effects toward the overall average. Big samples mostly keep their own signal; tiny samples get softened.

Try this: lower the bucket count and make the observed effect extreme. The pooled estimate moves back toward the overall effect.

Practical E2 flow

Use the lightest method that answers the question.

Start with fleet-level results, task-count weights, confidence intervals, and CUPED. Add adjusted regression when filters create imbalance. Save MLM for advanced cases with many small fleets.

Raw result: What did we observe?

Weighted result: What did most tasks experience?

CUPED: How did buckets perform vs expectation?

CI: How certain is the estimate?

Regression: Are we comparing similar conditions?

MLM: Are tiny fleets looking too extreme?
